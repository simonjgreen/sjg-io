If you have worked in IT for long enough, you have probably inherited an Access database.

You know the one.

It was built years ago by somebody in Finance, Operations, or HR. It started as a quick way to keep track of something that the corporate systems did not handle very well. Then it acquired a few forms, some reports, a bit of Visual Basic and, before anybody quite noticed, became essential to running the business.

The person who created it moved on.

Nobody really understood how it worked.

There was no documentation.

It lived on a shared drive with a filename like `Customer Database FINAL v6.accdb`.

Every few months it corrupted itself, stopped accepting new records, or produced a report with numbers that did not quite match Finance. At that point somebody from IT would be summoned to fix an application they had never built, had never approved and, until that morning, did not know existed.

It is easy to look back and blame Microsoft Access.

I do not think Access was the problem.

Access was brilliant.

It gave ordinary people the ability to solve real business problems without waiting six months for an IT project. It allowed somebody who understood the work to turn that understanding into a usable application.

The problem was not that people built things with Access.

The problem was that the cost of building software suddenly fell, while the way organisations governed software stayed exactly the same.

We are about to do it again.

Only this time, the things people create will not just store customer addresses and print reports.

They will call APIs, update production systems, communicate with customers, approve requests, move money, interpret policy, and make decisions on behalf of the business.

## We have been through this cycle before

Access was not the first wave of end-user software, and it certainly was not the last.

Excel macros gave people a way to automate calculations and repetitive work. Some of them became so important that entire companies depended on a spreadsheet maintained by one person who understood the formulas.

Access let those same people build small database applications.

SharePoint workflows allowed departments to digitise forms, approvals, and processes without commissioning a full application.

Low-code platforms such as Power Apps took things further. They made it possible to create genuinely useful internal applications with relatively little conventional development.

Each wave lowered the barrier to creating software.

Each created enormous value.

Each also created a new category of mess for somebody else to clean up later.

That does not mean the tools failed. Quite the opposite. They succeeded so well that organisations could not keep up with the volume of things being created.

The benefits arrived immediately and locally.

The debt arrived later and centrally.

A department saved hundreds of hours using a clever spreadsheet. That was visible straight away. Five years later, when the spreadsheet had become business-critical and its author had left, the risk belonged to IT.

Someone built an Access database in a week instead of waiting a year for a corporate system change. That was an entirely rational decision. The problem only appeared later, once twenty people relied on it and nobody could explain the business logic buried inside it.

We keep describing these things as failures of governance.

Often, they are really failures of scale.

The organisation had a process for governing ten large applications. It did not have a process for governing ten thousand small ones.

## AI changes the economics again

AI is not merely another low-code platform.

It changes the cost of producing software by another order of magnitude.

A person no longer needs to understand database design, formulas, scripting, or even the particular conventions of a low-code product. They can describe what they want in ordinary language and have a working version created for them.

It will not always be good.

It will not always be safe.

It may not even be particularly well designed.

But it will often be good enough to solve the immediate problem.

That is all Access had to be.

Imagine someone in Operations notices that the same information is being copied between three systems every morning. In the old world, they might raise a request with IT. It would be assessed, prioritised, funded, and eventually added to a delivery backlog.

In the new world, they describe the problem to an AI assistant before lunch.

The assistant finds the relevant APIs, creates the workflow, tests it against sample data, and gives them something usable that afternoon.

By the end of the week, twelve people are relying on it.

By the end of the month, it is part of how the department works.

Nobody ever called it a project.

There was no business case, project manager, steering committee, or formal launch. There may not even be a traditional application to point at.

It just appeared.

This is where I think the idea of the IT project starts to break down.

Projects made sense when software was expensive to create.

If a new system required a team of engineers, a six-month delivery timeline and a substantial investment, it made sense to wrap it in governance. You needed requirements, funding, milestones, and a clear decision to begin.

What happens when the first working version takes thirty minutes?

The need for governance has not disappeared. The economics that allowed governance to sit at the front of the process have.

You cannot put every AI-generated workflow through a three-month approval cycle. People will simply go around it, and they will be right to do so.

But you also cannot pretend these things are harmless just because they were quick to build.

An automation that took twenty minutes to create can still delete a customer record.

An agent assembled in an afternoon can still email thousands of people.

A workflow with no conventional code can still contain business logic that determines whether someone gets paid.

The effort required to build something has never been a reliable measure of its importance. AI is about to make that gap impossible to ignore.

## Software becomes disposable

For most of the history of enterprise technology, software was treated as an asset partly because it was expensive to produce.

You maintained it because rebuilding it would be costly.

You documented it because a replacement team would need to understand it.

You planned upgrades because the application was expected to survive for years.

That assumption is beginning to weaken.

When software can be generated quickly, rebuilding may be cheaper than maintaining.

A department may not upgrade its internal application. It may ask an AI to create a new one.

A workflow may exist for three weeks to support a temporary process and then be discarded.

Several teams may independently generate slightly different solutions to the same problem because creating another one is easier than discovering and adapting the first.

Software starts to become disposable.

That sounds liberating, and in many ways it is. There are thousands of valuable ideas inside businesses today that are not worth turning into traditional software projects. AI will make many of them viable.

But disposable does not mean consequence-free.

A disposable cup is simple because nothing else depends on it.

Software very quickly becomes connected to people, systems, data, and decisions. It may have been easy to create, but the moment someone starts relying on it, it has acquired weight.

The code itself might be disposable.

Its consequences are not.

## The new technical debt is not the code

This is where I think our existing language starts to fail us.

We tend to talk about AI-generated software as though the main risk is poor-quality code.

There will certainly be plenty of poor-quality code. I am just not convinced that will be the biggest problem.

Code can be reviewed.

Code can be tested.

Increasingly, code can simply be regenerated.

The harder questions are elsewhere.

Who owns this thing?

Who knows it exists?

Which identity does it run as?

What permissions has it been given?

Which systems can it access?

What data does it retain?

Which model does it use?

What instructions was it given?

Which tools and connectors can it call?

What happens when the person who created it leaves?

Which other workflows now depend on its output?

How would anyone know if it quietly started behaving differently?

Can we reconstruct why it made a particular decision six months ago?

Are there seven other agents doing almost the same thing?

These are not code-quality questions.

They are questions of ownership, authority, discoverability, and institutional memory.

The new technical debt will be hidden in prompts, agent definitions, tool permissions, workflow connections, embedded policies, and undocumented assumptions.

It will live in the gap between what an agent is technically capable of doing and what everyone believes it is supposed to do.

In some cases, the source code may be the least interesting part of the system.

The business logic will be spread across natural-language instructions, examples, knowledge sources, model behaviour, and the permissions granted to a collection of tools.

That is much harder to reason about than a badly written Visual Basic macro.

## Shadow AI will not look like Shadow IT

Shadow IT generally appeared because central IT could not move quickly enough.

People bought their own SaaS tools, created spreadsheets, and built Access databases because the official route was too slow, too expensive, or too disconnected from the actual work.

Shadow AI will emerge for a slightly different reason.

It will not just be that IT cannot build things quickly enough.

It will be that everybody can build things so quickly that nobody can keep count.

The problem moves from delivery capacity to visibility.

An organisation may have hundreds of approved applications and tens of thousands of small AI-created artefacts operating between them.

Some will be agents.

Some will be workflows.

Some will be scripts generated for a single task.

Some will be persistent instructions attached to a model.

Some will exist inside SaaS products.

Some will live on laptops.

Some will be assembled through chat and never formally named at all.

Our current application inventories are not designed for this world.

Neither are our architecture boards, project offices, or annual planning cycles.

They assume software has a beginning.

A project starts, something is delivered, and the organisation records what now exists.

AI-created software may have no obvious beginning. It will emerge continuously from the work itself.

## Banning it will fail

The obvious reaction will be to restrict creation.

That would be a mistake.

It was a mistake to treat every spreadsheet as a threat.

It was a mistake to assume every departmental database should have been replaced by a multimillion-pound enterprise system.

It would be an even bigger mistake to force every AI-created workflow through the same process as a major corporate application.

These tools create value precisely because they allow the people closest to a problem to solve it.

The answer cannot be to restore scarcity.

The answer has to be governance that works under abundance.

That means moving governance away from permission to create and towards the conditions under which created things can operate.

People should be able to experiment freely with low-risk data and actions.

The moment something connects to a production system, affects another person or begins making consequential decisions, stronger controls should appear automatically.

Identity should be built in.

Permissions should be explicit.

Tool calls should be observable.

Every persistent agent or workflow should have an owner.

Anything business-critical should use organisational credentials rather than somebody's personal account.

Systems should be discoverable without requiring a committee to approve every prototype.

Temporary things should expire unless someone deliberately keeps them.

Repeated solutions should be visible so that teams can reuse them rather than unknowingly creating the seventeenth version.

This is less like traditional project governance and more like building roads, signs, and traffic rules.

You do not approve every journey before someone gets in a car.

You create an environment in which millions of journeys can happen without constant central intervention.

That is the challenge enterprise technology teams now face.

Not how to stop people creating software.

How to make rampant software creation safe enough to become normal.

## The Access database was a warning

I do not think we should remember the Access era as a disaster.

An enormous amount of useful work was done in those databases.

They filled gaps that expensive corporate systems ignored. They allowed people to improve their own work. They proved that software creation did not have to belong exclusively to professional developers.

The mess came because our organisations did not adapt to the abundance Access created.

We treated each database as an isolated oddity rather than recognising that the cost of software production had changed.

Then we repeated the same pattern with spreadsheets, SharePoint workflows, and low-code platforms.

AI is the next wave, but it is not just a slightly easier version of what came before.

The scale is different.

The speed is different.

Most importantly, the software can act.

We are entering another Access database era, except this time the databases can read our policies, call our systems, make decisions, and talk to our customers.

The organisations that succeed will not be the ones that prevent this explosion.

They will not even necessarily be the ones that create the most.

They will be the ones that understand a fairly uncomfortable truth early enough:

When software becomes almost free to create, building it is no longer the hard part.

Knowing what exists, what it can do, who is responsible for it, and whether anyone should still trust it becomes the real work.
